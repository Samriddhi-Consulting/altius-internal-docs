# Last in Line

Dilip Sancheti keeps you waiting eleven minutes past the scheduled time, which you have decided not to read into, because you spent six weeks trying to get this meeting at all and eleven minutes is nothing compared to that. You spend the wait rereading your own notes for the third time that morning, not because they've changed since the first read but because rereading them is easier than sitting still. The waiting area doubles as a parts store, shelves of small machined components in labelled bins floor to ceiling, and somewhere behind a wall a lathe is running. You can't tell if it's cutting your order or someone else's, and that not knowing is, in miniature, the whole reason you're here.

---

**How it used to run**

You are a buyer at a mid-size manufacturer, and for two years, the precision components you source from Sancheti Engineering Works have shown up more or less when promised, at more or less the price agreed, without you having to think about the relationship at all. It was, by the standards of supplier relationships, a quietly good one. You inherited the account from a colleague who described it, almost fondly, as "the one vendor I never have to worry about," and for a long time that description held.

That changed roughly eight months ago, quietly enough that you didn't notice the pattern until it was already established. Deliveries started slipping by a few days, then a week, then two. Calls to your usual contact on their ops team went unreturned more often than not. When you finally did get someone on the phone, the answers were vague in a specific way, "we're working on it," "should be out by Friday," Friday coming and going without the shipment or an explanation. You told yourself, for the first month or two, that this was a temporary blip, a busy season, a staffing gap that would resolve itself. You have stopped telling yourself that.

---

**What finally got you in the room**

Three weeks ago, a delivery that was supposed to arrive on a Tuesday arrived the following Monday, and your own production line sat partially idle for four of those days because there was no substitute part on short notice. Your own operations head asked you, not unkindly but directly, why a two-year relationship that used to run itself was suddenly costing the company real money. You didn't have a good answer. That is the day you stopped asking for updates and started asking for a meeting with the owner directly. It took three more weeks and, you suspect, a favor called in by your own manager, who has known Dilip's father for years, to actually get on his calendar.

You don't know, walking in, whether Dilip is even aware of how bad it's gotten on your end. His ops manager, Suresh, the person you've been chasing for two months, reports to him, but you have no way of knowing whether any of your complaints ever made it up the chain in a form Dilip would recognize as urgent. It's entirely possible he thinks everything is fine. It's also possible he knows exactly what's happening and has simply decided your account isn't worth fixing it for.

---

**What you know about Dilip**

What little you know about Dilip himself comes from your manager, who has known the family for years. Second generation, took over from his father a decade ago, grew the shop from a dozen machines to nearly forty. Well liked in the industry, the kind of small manufacturer other small manufacturers speak well of at trade events, not because he's the cheapest but because he's historically been the most reliable. There's a story your manager told you once, about Dilip personally driving a replacement part two hundred kilometers overnight for a customer's emergency, years ago, back when the shop was small enough that he still ran deliveries himself sometimes. Whether that story is still true of the business he runs now, forty machines and presumably a very different set of pressures, is exactly the kind of thing you won't know until you're in the room.

That reputation is presumably part of why your company chose him in the first place, and part of what makes the last eight months feel less like a business problem and more like a small betrayal of something you didn't realize you were counting on.

The eleven minutes you waited outside come back to you now, and you turn them over differently than you did at the time. He kept you waiting, but he also agreed to see you at all, on a weekday morning, in the middle of a production run, without your manager sitting in to soften it for either of you. You don't know yet whether that's a good sign or just good manners. Your operations head, for what it's worth, has stopped asking for delivery updates and started asking, most Monday mornings now, whether you have a plan B, a question you have so far answered with a version of "working on it" that sounds uncomfortably like the answers you've been getting from Suresh.

---

**The arithmetic you carry in**

What you do know, because you looked into it after the third missed delivery, is that Sancheti Engineering Works picked up a much larger client roughly eight months ago, a name you recognize from the industry, the kind of account that would represent a serious jump in volume for a shop with forty machines. You don't know for certain that this is connected to your own slipping priority. The timing is suggestive enough that you'd be surprised if it wasn't.

Here is the arithmetic you carry in, quietly, because it matters more to you than to him: your company represents, by your own estimate, somewhere around eight to ten percent of Sancheti's total output. You have been a customer for six years, longer than whoever the new account is. You pay on time, every time, without exception, a fact you have half a mind to lead with and have talked yourself out of twice already this morning, because you suspect every unhappy customer who has ever sat in that chair opened with some version of "but we've always paid on time."

None of that has, so far, translated into priority, which tells you something uncomfortable about how priority actually gets decided in a business like this. It isn't tenure. It probably isn't loyalty either, not on its own. It's almost certainly volume, and on volume alone, you are not going to win this conversation, at least not by making the case that you deserve better treatment than a client several times your size.

What you haven't worked out yet is what you actually want to ask for, beyond "please fix this." A dedicated production slot, even a small one, ring-fenced against the next big rush order. A named point of contact who actually returns calls, since Suresh, whatever is happening with him, has clearly stopped being that person. Some kind of early-warning system, so a slipping delivery becomes a phone call three days out instead of a surprise on the day itself. You have three ideas and no sense yet of which one Dilip would actually be able to say yes to inside his own operation, running forty machines and presumably several accounts exactly like yours competing for the same capacity.

---

**Your own stake**

There is also a smaller, more personal calculation you haven't said out loud to anyone. You inherited this account and, until eight months ago, got credit for how little trouble it caused. Your name is the one on the vendor scorecard that finance reviews every quarter, and last quarter's on-time delivery number for Sancheti dropped low enough that a colleague in a different department asked you, in a tone that was almost but not quite joking, whether you'd picked a bad supplier. You didn't pick badly. You picked well, six years ago, and something changed on the other end that had nothing to do with your judgment. Whether that distinction survives contact with your own leadership if this meeting goes nowhere is a separate question, and not one you plan to raise with Dilip.

You do not have a real alternative supplier lined up. You've made two calls to other shops in the last month, mostly to have something to say if this conversation goes badly, but requalifying a new precision supplier takes months, sometimes longer, testing tolerances and running trial batches before a single production part could actually ship, and neither of those calls has produced anything close to a workable option yet. Walking in and threatening to leave would be, at this point, a bluff Dilip could probably see through in real time, and you suspect he's had this exact conversation with other unhappy customers before and knows exactly what an empty threat sounds like, the particular cadence of someone reciting a line they rehearsed in the car on the way over.

What you do have is history, a specific and recent example of what the slippage actually cost you, on both a human and a numbers level, and a genuine, uncomfortable question you haven't fully worked out how to ask without it sounding like a complaint about people who aren't in the room. You would rather talk about the pattern than list grievances against Suresh by name. You suspect, though you can't be sure yet, that naming names might be exactly what Dilip is braced for, having sat through it before with other accounts, and that starting there would close the conversation down before it's had a chance to open.

Your own manager's advice, delivered on the phone last night in about four sentences, was blunter than you expected: "Don't go in there to complain. Go in there to find out what he'd need to see to trust you with priority again. Those are different conversations, and only one of them gets you anything."

You've turned that advice over more than once since. It's easier said than done when the thing you actually want to say, walking in, is some version of "why does it take a production line sitting idle for four days to get a phone call returned." You know that sentence, however true, would land as an accusation rather than an opening, and you're fairly sure an accusation is not what gets you back to the front of anyone's queue. What you don't yet know is what the alternative opening actually sounds like, or whether you'll find it once you're sitting across from him instead of rehearsing it alone in a car.

---

**In his office**

His office sits above the workshop floor, and through the window behind him you can see two of the machines that presumably used to run parts for your account and, these days, seem to be running something else more often than not. A row of finished parts sits crated near the loading bay below, stenciled with a customer code you don't recognize, and you find yourself doing the arithmetic again without meaning to, wondering how many of those crates represent the account that quietly moved you down the list.

He gestures you into the chair, checks his watch once, not rudely, more like a habit, and folds his hands on the desk.
