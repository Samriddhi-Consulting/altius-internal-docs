# The Market Launch Reset

Meher Sabharwal has the launch deck open on the screen behind her, still on the title slide, and she has not looked at it once.

The room is the small one on the fourth floor, the one with the broken blind nobody has fixed since March. She got here early. There is a printout in front of her, six months of Sabza numbers pulled straight out of the system rather than out of anybody's summary, and she has been through it twice with a pencil. Circles, mostly. A few question marks.

When you come in she moves the printout aside and says she has ten minutes of things to say, and then she wants to hear from you.

She takes about four.

The board meets Thursday morning. Rs 14 crore has gone into Sabza since February. Sales are running at roughly half of what the launch plan said they would be by now, and the next phase needs another Rs 9 crore she has not yet asked for. Her view, stated plainly and without any drama, is that the company should stop. Pull back to two cities, hold the line, stop spending against a product the market has looked at and declined. She has watched a launch die slowly before and she does not intend to preside over another one.

Then she says she is willing to be wrong about this. She would rather be wrong now than in November. She asks what you think.

---

**What Sabza was supposed to be**

Sabza went into six cities in February. Mumbai, Delhi NCR, Bengaluru, Pune, Hyderabad and Chennai. Three hundred and fifty millilitres, low sugar, added electrolytes, basil seed suspended in it so the bottle has visible texture on the shelf. Rs 90. A regular soft drink of the same size sits at Rs 40 to Rs 50. The imported functional drinks Sabza was benchmarked against sit between Rs 120 and Rs 150.

The plan was built around urban professionals aged 25 to 40. People who had started reading labels, who drank something on the way to work or after the gym, who would pay a premium for less sugar without wanting to think too hard about why. The media plan followed. Digital first, heavy on video, distribution weighted towards modern trade and convenience, with quick commerce treated as a secondary channel that would take care of itself.

Awareness came fast. By month five, aided awareness across the six cities was 41% against a plan of 30%. The launch films did well. 2.3 million views, engagement at 6.1%, a number the agency has mentioned in every review since. Click-through from those films to a purchase page is running at 0.4%.

Trial is where the plan first came apart. 12% of the people who know Sabza have bought it. The plan said 22%. Of the people who bought it once, 19% bought it again. The plan said 40%. 7% have bought a third time.

Nobody disputes these numbers. What nobody agrees on is what they mean.

The agency has a theory about the digital gap and has presented it twice. Their argument is that the films were built for reach, the reach worked, and the drop between watching and buying is the normal cost of a category that has to explain itself before it can sell. They want a second flight built for conversion, with product pages and a retargeting layer that was cut from the original plan on budget grounds.

The number underneath that theory is worth sitting with. 2.3 million people watched. Roughly 9,200 clicked. Nobody traced what happened to those 9,200 after the click, because the tracking was set up to measure the click and then stop.

**Three people who will not be in the room**

Rohit Bhatia has been National Sales Head for eleven years and his position has not moved since April. Sabza is overpriced. Rs 90 is a barrier, the shopper picks the bottle up and puts it back, and the whole thing would look different at Rs 70. He wants a promotional price for one quarter and he wants it before the festive season. He has said this in three consecutive reviews and he has started saying it in a tone that suggests he is tired of saying it. His field team has stopped bringing Sabza up in monthly calls unless somebody asks.

Anuja Desai in finance is not against Sabza. She is against the shape of the ask. Rs 14 crore has gone out, the return on it cannot be demonstrated, and she will not support a further Rs 9 crore until somebody tells her what specifically will be different this time and how anyone would know. She has asked twice for a measurement plan. Neither version convinced her. The second one, she said in the meeting, was a list of dashboards.

Trilok Distributors handle Mumbai and Pune. Their margin on Sabza is 14%. On the competing lines they carry it runs between 18% and 22%. Dhruv Kotecha, who runs Trilok, has been polite about this for five months. Last week he told the regional manager he would be reviewing shelf commitment for the next cycle, which is the kind of sentence distributors use when they mean something firmer.

Meher will bring all three of them into the room with her. None of them will be sitting in it. She has told her assistant she needs to know by Wednesday evening what she is walking into the board with, which leaves this conversation and not a great deal after it.

---

**The channel picture**

The channel picture is not what the plan assumed.

| Channel | Share of volume | Plan | Repeat rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quick commerce | 31% | 12% | 34% |
| Modern trade | 34% | 45% | 16% |
| General trade and convenience | 35% | 43% | 11% |

Quick commerce was supposed to be the small one. It is carrying nearly a third of the volume and its repeat rate is roughly double what modern trade manages. The four bottle multipack, which was only ever listed on quick commerce because the packaging team could not get it approved for shelf in time, accounts for 22% of what quick commerce sells.

The retail audit in June found something else. In three of the five modern trade chains, Sabza is sitting in the juice chiller. It was meant to sit alongside sports and energy drinks. Where it is correctly placed, off-take is still running at about 60% of plan, so placement does not close the gap on its own, though the chains with the wrong placement are noticeably worse.

You went to one of the wrongly placed stores yourself in July, a large format outlet in Andheri, and stood near the chiller for about twenty minutes on a Saturday evening. Four people picked up a bottle. Three read the back label. Two put it back and took something else off the same shelf. The one who bought it was already holding a basket with two other health products in it. The store manager, when you asked, said Sabza had come to him in the juice planogram and he had no instruction to move it, and that nobody from the company had been into his store since the launch week sampling in February.

---

**What people say about the taste**

The consumer feedback has generated the most argument and the least agreement.

The research vendor coded 600 unprompted comments from the review widget and the customer care line. Taste came up in 38% of them, more than any other theme. The two phrases that recur are that it is not sweet enough, and that there is something that stays in the mouth afterwards. The word people keep reaching for is aftertaste.

That reading has a problem. The pre-launch concept test put taste acceptability at 72% against an internal benchmark of 70%. It cleared. And when the vendor split the feedback by whether the person had bought Sabza more than once, taste barely appears in the repeat buyer comments at all.

The repeat buyers are worth looking at closely, because they are not who the plan described. They skew female, late twenties to mid forties, and the pattern in their purchase data is household rather than personal. They buy multipacks. They reorder on a cycle of roughly two to three weeks. Several of the comments in that group mention children, or a husband who has been told to cut down on sugar, or a fridge that needs restocking. Nobody in the marketing team wrote a line of copy with any of these people in mind.

Meher has seen the same deck you have. What she has not decided is whether it means anything.

---

**What you inherited**

You have had this brand for four months. You did not write the launch plan. You inherited it in April along with two other categories and a team of three, and you spent the first six weeks trying to work out why the numbers coming out of quick commerce did not match the story everybody in the building was telling.

The multipack is a small illustration of how the launch has actually gone. It exists because a packaging specification missed a shelf-ready deadline in January, and quick commerce was the only channel that could take the format without one. Nobody planned it. Nobody costed it separately. It now moves more than a fifth of the volume in the channel that is beating its plan by the widest margin. When you raised this in the June review, two people said it was interesting and the conversation moved on.

You can recommend. You cannot decide. The Rs 9 crore is Meher's to ask for and the board's to approve, and if she walks into Thursday saying she wants to pause, that is what will happen. Rohit will not run a price promotion because you asked him to. Anuja will not release budget on the strength of a good argument in a meeting room. Dhruv will not hold his shelf out of goodwill.

Pausing is not a soft word either. Pulling back to two cities means telling four sales teams to stop, giving up listings that took five months to win, and explaining to Dhruv Kotecha that the brand he has been carrying at 14% is going quiet. Stock already in the trade has to go somewhere. The multipack has no home outside quick commerce. Whatever the board note calls it, the market will read it as a withdrawal, and a listing in a chain that has already dropped you costs more the second time. Meher knows all of that. It has not changed her view.

The options have not changed since April. Spend more against awareness and conversion. Change who the product is for. Change what it says it does. Change some combination of the product, the price and where it is sold. Or stop expanding and fix the experience before another rupee goes behind it. More than one of them can be true at the same time.

---

**The room**

Meher checks her phone, turns it face down, and pushes the printout an inch towards you.

Behind her the deck is still on the title slide. She has not turned round to it once.

The blind is still broken. There is about forty minutes before her next meeting.

She turns the printout around so it is facing you and asks you to start wherever you want.
