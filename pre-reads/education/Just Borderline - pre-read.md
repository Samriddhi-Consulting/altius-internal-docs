# Just Borderline

The OPD is already full by the time you reach the general medicine desk. Slippers and sandals shuffling on the floor, a ceiling fan pushing warm air around, the token numbers clicking over on the board above the door. Somewhere a child is crying and being shushed. You have a stack of files and a consultant who is running three rooms at once. This is the rhythm of the morning: fast, crowded, and nobody in it expecting their day to change.

You are a final-year medical intern on your general medicine posting. Three weeks in. You have learned how to move a queue, how to read a report quickly, how to keep the consultant's clinic from falling behind. The clinical facts you know cold by now. What the posting has not taught you yet is the part where you sit across from one person and say a true thing they did not come here to hear.

The next file on the stack belongs to a man who thinks he is about to be told he is fine.

---

**The Man in Front of You**

Manoj Patil is forty-four. He runs a small transport business, Patil Roadways: two trucks, one hired driver, and himself behind the wheel on the long runs to Nagpur and Nashik. He is the only earner. There is a wife at home and a daughter in the eleventh standard he talks about sending to college.

He did not come here feeling sick. He came because his bank wants a medical fitness certificate before it clears a loan for a third truck. To him this visit is paperwork. A formality between him and a bigger business. He has a driver double-parked outside and a load that has to move by afternoon, and he would like to be out of here in ten minutes with a signature.

He is easy to like. Talkative, practical, quick with a joke. He measures his life in diesel, EMIs, and kilometres. He is not a doctor and does not pretend to be one, and he has walked in expecting good news the way you expect the sun.

He shakes your hand on the way in and asks if you are from around here. That is the kind of man he is. He will make small talk with the person who is about to change his year, because he has no idea yet that this is what you are about to do.

His morning was ordinary. Tea at the stand near the transport office, a call to the driver about the afternoon load, the drive over with the radio on. He parked badly because he meant to be quick. Everything about how he arrived says a man who expects to leave as the same person who walked in. You are the only one in this room who already knows that is not how the next twenty minutes go.

---

**What the Reports Say**

The bloods drawn earlier are back, and they are not a formality.

His HbA1c is 9.2 percent. His fasting glucose is around 215. You know what that means: this is not the grey zone, not prediabetes, not something to watch. This is Type 2 diabetes, clearly, already established. The word "borderline" does not apply to these numbers, however much anyone in this room would like it to.

Read back through the history and the signs were there, quietly, for a while. More thirst than the heat explained. Getting up at night to pass urine. A tiredness he put down to long hauls. Some blurring on the highway after dark that he has been squinting through. He never called any of it a symptom. To him it was age, and the road, and summer. He is not careless about his own body. He is busy, and a body stays patient about its warnings right up until the morning it stops being patient. None of that changes the number in front of you now.

None of this is a mystery to you clinically. The management, in time, is routine medicine: a tablet, a diet sheet, a plan, a rhythm of check-ups. Half the adults in the waiting room outside are living with some version of it, and doing fine. That is not what this conversation is about. This conversation is about whether the man holding the loan papers walks out understanding what has actually happened to him, or walks out relieved and wrong.

---

**Why This Is Hard**

He is not braced. That is the whole difficulty in one line.

People who fear bad news often arrive already half expecting it, some guard already up. Manoj has no guard up at all. He came for a signature. The distance between what he expects in the next few minutes and what you are about to say is enormous, and you are the one who has to carry him across it without dropping him and without lying to him about where he is standing.

There is a truck outside. There is a load and a deadline and a driver waiting. All of that is going to be sitting in the room with you, pulling him toward the door, while you try to make a diagnosis real to a man who has every reason to wish it away.

---

**The Certificate**

There is one more knot in this, and it is worth seeing before you sit down. Manoj did not come for a diagnosis. He came for a signature, a piece of paper that stands between him and a bigger business. Somewhere in the next few minutes he is going to realise that the certificate he wanted may now be tangled up with the news you are giving him, and he is going to want you to make that problem go away.

You cannot, not today. Whether he gets his fitness certificate, and on what terms, is not a promise you can hand across the table, any more than you can promise him the diagnosis is smaller than it is. Some of what he asks you for, you will have to hold honestly without giving it to him. That is hard to do when the man asking has a driver waiting and an instalment due at the end of the month. It is still the job.

---

**What He Will Reach For**

When it lands, he will not go quiet. He will bargain.

He will try to shrink it. "Borderline only, na?" He will offer you the smallest possible version of the problem so he can accept that one instead. "Just diet, I'll cut the sugar in my tea and it's finished." He may decide the report is mixed up, because he feels completely fine, and feeling fine is the only evidence his body has given him.

Watch for the other thing too, the quieter failure: the nod. If you bury him in numbers and complications and long words, he will not argue. He will say "haan haan, theek hai doctor," agree with all of it, check his watch, and absorb none of it. A patient who agrees with everything has often understood nothing. The certificate-and-signature version of this conversation is easy and useless. He leaves polite. He also leaves unchanged.

---

**The Pull of the Queue**

Everything about this room rewards speed. There are thirty people outside who also need to be seen. The consultant wants the clinic moving. There is a decent part of you that does not want to hold a hardworking man back from his afternoon load, and there is a tired part of you that would find it easier to let him keep his "borderline" and reach for the next file.

That pull is the quiet enemy in this scene. Not cruelty, just the current of a busy morning carrying you toward the version of this that takes ninety seconds and helps no one. Slowing down for a few minutes with this one man is a choice you have to make on purpose, against the room. It will not feel like there is time. There rarely is. It is still the whole difference between a patient who heard you and a patient who nodded at you.

---

**Underneath**

A reaction this stubborn is rarely only about today.

Most people meet a word like diabetes already carrying a private picture of it: someone they watched live with it, or not live with it. What that picture is for Manoj, and what he is actually afraid of losing, is not written in the file. It will only come out if something in how you talk to him makes it safe to say, and it will close again fast if he decides you are performing concern rather than feeling it. You do not need to guess it in advance. You need to leave enough room in the conversation for it to arrive.

His bargaining is worth hearing as more than denial. "Can't it just be diet" is a question about his life, not only about his treatment. The man asking it drives for a living.

---

**What You Carry In**

Not a script. There is no sentence that makes this land softly and there is no trick that makes it painless.

The honest version sits between two easy escapes. One is comfort: agree that it is probably borderline, promise that diet alone will sort it out, send him off happy. That is kind for ninety seconds and a betrayal by next year. The other is force: frighten him with everything that can go wrong, the eyes and the kidneys and the feet, until he is too scared to argue. That does not make him understand. It makes him shut the door. What is left, the harder middle, is plain words, said warmly, checked to see if they actually reached him, with the truth kept intact and his fear given somewhere to go.

You also carry the limits of your own coat. You are the intern. You can open this honestly, sit with what it does to him, and set up what comes next, a proper follow-up, the consultant who will decide the actual treatment. You cannot promise him a cure. You cannot promise the loan certificate. You should not be writing him a lifelong prescription in a corridor. Knowing what is yours to say and what belongs to the consultant is part of doing this well.

None of that means leaving him with nothing. Between a false promise and a cold referral there is a real thing you can give him: a clear picture of where he stands, drawn honestly, and a first step small enough that he will actually take it. That is worth more to him this morning than either comfort or a warning, and it sits entirely within your reach as the intern in the room.

---

**The Room You Are Entering**

A small consulting room off the main OPD. A table, two chairs, a curtain half pulled for the last patient. His file is open in front of you with the report on top. Through the door, the queue and the fan and the token board carry on.

Manoj sits down across from you, already half rising to leave in his head, papers ready for your signature. He is relaxed. He is smiling. He has no idea.

The first thing he says will be some version of "everything fine, na, doctor?"

Everything is not fine. The next few minutes are yours. You have twenty turns before the debrief, and a man who came in for a signature and is going to leave with something much heavier, whether or not he leaves with the truth.

He is looking at you now, pen practically already in your hand.
