# A Reasonable Request

Arjun Sathe is standing at the security gate at ten past nine, holding a
visitor pass he should not have needed.

He has been the key account manager for Tarani Autocomp for four years. He has
walked through this gate perhaps thirty times. But his plant pass expired in
April, nobody renewed it, and so he waits at the window while a guard
photographs his driving licence and types his name in one letter at a time. He
does not complain. He takes out a spiral notebook, the kind with a rubber band
around it, and writes something down while he waits.

You are the plant operations lead. Your phone has three messages from him from
last week, and one from the week before that, all asking for the same fifteen
minutes. You moved him twice. Once for a compressor trip that took out half a
shift, once because you forgot. Today you are giving him the fifteen minutes.

---

**The plant, and the grade**

The site compounds engineering thermoplastics. Nylons mostly, with a smaller
line for polypropylene compounds that came in three years ago and has never
quite paid for itself. Two compounding lines, twin-screw, with the larger one
running at about 2.2 tonnes an hour when it behaves.

Tarani takes a 33 percent glass-filled PA66, natural, no colour package. It
goes into an air-intake manifold assembly that sits under the bonnet of a
passenger car, which means it lives at temperature, in vibration, next to
things that are hot and oily. Tarani moulds the component and ships it to Arvo
Motors, who build it into a platform that has been in production for six
years.

They take about 40 tonnes a month. At roughly 285 rupees a kilo that is a
little over 1.1 crore of revenue every month, close to 14 crore a year. By
volume Tarani is your third-largest customer on the nylon line. By margin
contribution they are second, because glass-filled grades carry better
economics than the unfilled material that makes up most of your tonnage.

There is one more thing about the grade, and it matters more than the tonnage
does.

Automotive components are approved through a process called PPAP, the
production part approval process. Before Tarani could sell that manifold to
Arvo, they had to submit a package proving the part performs: dimensional
results, material certifications, durability and thermal cycling data, process
capability studies. The compound they used is named in that submission. It is
named by grade, by supplier, and by manufacturing location.

Which means Tarani cannot change compound the way they change stationery
suppliers. A different resin means a Level 3 resubmission. New material
testing, new validation on moulded parts, and then Arvo's engineering team has
to sign it off on their own timeline. The industry number for that loop is nine
to fourteen months, and the customer does not control the clock. Arvo does.

You have known this for years without ever quite saying it out loud. It is the
reason you have never worried much about Tarani.

---

**How the account came to be yours**

Eleven years ago Tarani was a much smaller company running two moulding
machines and a hope. They came to the plant because their existing compounder
had shut a line and left them stranded, and the plant took the business at a
price that made sense to nobody, on the argument that a growing customer is
worth carrying. Somebody was right about that. Tarani now runs eighteen
machines across two units and supplies four component families to three
different vehicle makers.

The price has been corrected since, in stages, without much drama. Last
February there was a conversation about a 6 rupee a kilo increase on the back
of caprolactam movement, and Devesh Kulkarni pushed back for six weeks before
settling at 4.50. It was a normal negotiation. He grumbled, you held, he
signed.

That is roughly the shape of the whole relationship. Eleven years of orders
placed, material delivered, invoices paid within terms give or take a fortnight.
No joint projects. No visits from their engineering team to your line, and none
from your development group to theirs. The two companies have made a great deal
of money from each other while remaining, in every meaningful sense, strangers.

---

**What has gone wrong**

Three things, and none of them individually looks like much.

In March, a dispatch went out eleven days late. A bearing failure on the main
extruder gearbox, a spare that had to come from Pune, and a queue of orders
behind it that all slipped together. Tarani was told on day four.

In May, a second dispatch went six days late. This one was your own planning.
An appliance customer on firm allocation had a volume call-off you had agreed
to protect, and Tarani's campaign got pushed to the back of the cycle. When the
material finally reached Haldia port road, Tarani's stores had a single day of
compound left. Their plant manager called your logistics coordinator at eleven
at night to confirm the truck had left. Nobody escalated it to you.

In June, a batch came off the line with relative viscosity at the low edge of
the specification window. Inside the window, technically compliant, signed off
by Meenal Rao in quality and released on concession. You saw the paperwork. It
was one of forty documents you signed that week.

Tarani accepted the batch. They did not complain, they did not raise a
deviation, and they did not ask for a credit. That struck you as decent of
them at the time.

It is worth remembering how that release actually happened, because it took
four minutes. Meenal came to your office with the certificate of analysis and
the trend chart, said the viscosity was low but inside limits and the
alternative was holding 40 tonnes for a week, and asked whether to release.
You asked whether the glass content was fine. She said it was. You signed.

Under-hood nylon that runs at the bottom of its viscosity window is not a
quality failure. It is a part that may behave slightly differently in the mould
and slightly differently after a thousand hours at temperature. Your customer's
process engineers would know that better than you do. Nobody from Tarani rang
to discuss it, which is why it left your mind entirely by the following Monday.

---

**Things you have noticed and not thought about**

Tarani's rolling forecast for the next quarter came in nine days late this
month. When it arrived, the volumes were about 15 percent below their own trend
line, with a one-line note at the bottom saying figures were under review.

In April, they asked for a copy of the raw material specification sheet for the
compound, along with lot traceability documentation going back twelve months.
The request came by email, addressed to nobody in particular, and said it was
for their records. Your quality team sent it.

And the person on your monthly call has changed. For three years it was Devesh
Kulkarni from their purchase department, who talked about prices and rarely
about anything else. Since May it has been someone from their engineering
group, a woman who asks careful questions about process capability and takes a
lot of notes.

You noticed each of these. You did not put them next to each other.

---

**What is at stake for you**

Your own year is not going well, and it is going badly in a specific way.

The works manager wants conversion cost per tonne down 4 percent, and you are
at 1.2 percent with the year a third gone. Everything that gets you there
depends on long runs. Every changeover on the big line costs you about six
hours of production time and around 4 lakh in purge material that goes to
regrind or waste. Your OEE number is the one that appears on the slide the
works manager takes to the regional review, and his name is on that slide next
to yours.

The monthly production plan locks on Friday. Three days from now.

So when Arjun asks for what he is about to ask for, you already know what it
costs you. A dedicated campaign slot for Tarani means an extra changeover in
the cycle, possibly two. A buffer stock of 60 tonnes means about 1.7 crore of
working capital sitting in your warehouse in foil-lined bags, because PA66 is
hygroscopic and does not tolerate a casual storage regime. It means somebody in
finance asking why your inventory days went up in a year when you promised they
would come down.

You have already deferred two preventive maintenance windows to hit your
numbers. You know exactly what that will cost you eventually. Saying yes to
Arjun is not free, and you are not in a position to pretend otherwise.

There is also the matter of how the plan actually gets made. Every Wednesday
your planning coordinator, a patient man named Sujoy Ghosh, builds the campaign
sequence from the order book and the forecast file, and every Wednesday he
optimises for the fewest changeovers he can get away with. He is doing what you
have asked him to do. The consequence is that a customer with steady, modest,
predictable volumes gets sequenced wherever it hurts the schedule least, which
is usually last. Tarani has been last four times in seven months.

Nobody decided that. It is simply what the rule produces.

---

**About Arjun**

He is careful rather than smooth. He does not oversell, and when he does not
know something he says so, which is rarer than it should be in his job. He
came up through technical service before he moved to accounts, so he can hold a
conversation about melt temperature without needing an interpreter.

He is also, today, slightly wound up. Not aggressive. Wound up in the way of
someone who has rehearsed an opening and is worried it will come out wrong.

He asked for this meeting three times.

He has framed what he wants as a scheduling matter, because a scheduling matter
is a thing a plant can actually deliver, and because asking for something
concrete feels more professional than arriving with a vague worry. Whether the
scheduling request is the whole of it is a different question. He has spent
more time with this customer than anyone else in the company, and people who
spend that much time with a customer usually know things they have not
organised into a request yet.

Or he knows exactly what is happening and is choosing where to start.

---

**The last thing worth knowing**

There is a conversation the industry has been having for two years that has
never quite reached your desk. Platforms get re-sourced. Model years turn over.
New programmes go out to tender with new material requirements, thinner walls,
tighter warpage tolerances, higher continuous-use temperatures. Your development
team has talked, in the abstract, about what the next generation of this grade
family would take. Nobody from Tarani has ever sat in that conversation with
you.

The plant has never been part of a customer's product development. You make
what is on the specification and you make it consistently, and for eleven
years that has been enough to keep a customer who cannot easily leave.

---

Arjun comes through the gate at nine twenty. He has the notebook in his hand
and a laptop bag over one shoulder, and he stops to sign the visitor register
in the reception area, which takes another two minutes.

The meeting room on the first floor has the air conditioning on too high and a
whiteboard nobody has cleaned since the last audit. There is a jug of water and
two glasses. Through the window you can see trucks queued at the weighbridge.

He sits down, puts the notebook on the table, and does not open it.
