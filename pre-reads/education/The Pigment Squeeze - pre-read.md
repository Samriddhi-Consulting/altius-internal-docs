# The Pigment Squeeze

The conference room on the third floor of the plant still smells of fresh paint from last week's touch-up, and underneath it, day-old coffee. Outside the window, the industrial belt is already at full noise by nine, tankers idling at the gate, the smell of solvent drifting in from the coating lines two buildings over. You are twenty minutes early. Alok Bhandari is already there, phone face down on the table, jacket slung over the back of his chair like he has been coming to this room for years. He probably has.

"Traffic wasn't bad," he says, glancing at his watch anyway. "Good. We have a flight to catch tonight."

---

**How You Got This Chair**

You are six months into the job. Assistant Manager, Procurement. This was supposed to be Ritu Sharma's meeting. She has run every pigment negotiation for the plant since before you joined, and everyone from the plant head to the finance team defers to her on supplier calls without being asked to. She was meant to sit beside you today, mostly to watch, occasionally to nudge. Her flight to the head office review in Mumbai got moved up two hours this morning. She left you a WhatsApp message with a number circled twice and one line underneath it: *Don't cross this. Call me if you need to, but I'll be in the air by two.*

You have wanted a seat at exactly this kind of table since your first week on the trainee program, sat through two negotiations as a note taker, and asked Ritu more than once when she would let you run one yourself. This is not quite what you had in mind. There is no version of today where you call her mid meeting without it reading, to Alok at least, as a young hire who cannot make a decision alone, and there is no version where you guess wrong on the ceiling and it does not follow you into your next performance review. Ritu trusts you enough to leave. That is either a vote of confidence or a coincidence of bad timing, and you will not know which until this is over.

---

**Belmoore and the File**

Belmoore Chemicals has supplied titanium dioxide pigment to this plant for six years. Reliable. Never missed a delivery window, not once, even during the shipping delays that hit half the industry two years ago. The outgoing contract is unremarkable in every way an outgoing contract can be: four hundred and eighty metric tons a year, two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars a ton, forty five day credit. Three years ago, when Ritu first signed with Belmoore, the price was closer to eighteen hundred dollars. Last year's renewal barely changed a line, because for one quiet year the feedstock market behaved itself. You read the file twice last night and came away thinking this would be the easy one.

It will not be the easy one.

Titanium dioxide is not a glamorous material to build a career around, and you have said as much to your college friends more than once, half joking. But it is the whitest white anyone has found a way to manufacture at scale, and without it the plant's entire premium coatings line, the one with the highest margin per litre of anything the firm makes, simply does not exist. Swap it for a cheaper filler and the paint goes chalky within a year of sun exposure. There is no substitute grade that performs the same, not at a price anyone would pay. That is why this negotiation matters more than its line item on a spreadsheet would suggest, and why Ritu insisted on being here in person rather than dialling in.

---

**What Alok Opens With**

Alok opens his laptop, turns it so you can both see the screen, and gets there before you can say good morning. "You've seen what feedstock's done this year." It isn't a question. Ilmenite and rutile ore, the raw material behind every kilogram of TiO2 pigment made anywhere in the world, have been climbing since January, and Belmoore's plants have felt it in their own cost base long before it showed up in anyone's spot price index. He walks you through a chart of quarterly averages that have crept from just above two thousand dollars a ton to nearly two thousand six hundred, and mentions, almost in passing, that Belmoore's own price hike notices this year have added up to something in the four hundred and fifty to five hundred dollar range per ton across the portfolio. He adds, more quietly, that output at two of Belmoore's own plants has slipped nearly ten percent this year against last, ore supply being what it is. Belmoore runs the chloride route, he reminds you, the process that leans harder on high grade rutile ore rather than the cheaper ilmenite that sulphate route producers can get away with, which is precisely why their costs have moved faster than the market average this year. "We've held your account back from most of that. This is the year we can't."

He wants two things, and he wants them in the same sentence so neither sounds negotiable on its own. First, a new price built on a formula: a base cost plus a quarterly feedstock index, instead of a flat number fixed for a year. He sketches the math for you on a notepad, unprompted, as if showing his work will make it feel less like an ask. Base cost, he says, plus index movement times a weighting he does not fully explain, recalculated every quarter. Second, a three year term instead of one, "so we both stop having this conversation every twelve months." He says the second part like a favor. A formula this size, he adds, only makes sense to Belmoore's regional office if it is locked in for years, not renegotiated every time the ore market hiccups.

---

**Ritu's Note and What the File Leaves Out**

Ritu's note put your ceiling at two thousand four hundred dollars a ton, weighted average across the year, and told you nothing longer than eighteen months goes through without the CFO's sign off, full stop. She did not tell you how to get there. She trusts you to have read the file, and mostly, you have. What the file does not tell you is why Alok, specifically, needs this contract signed today rather than next week. You notice he has checked the time twice more since you sat down, and once, cutting himself off mid sentence, texted someone back in under ten seconds. Whatever is waiting for him at the airport tonight, or at head office tomorrow, is not part of the file. It might matter. It might not.

You know a few things he probably does not expect a six month hire to know. The plant's production schedule has a coating run booked in eighteen days that needs pigment in stock nine days before that, which is tighter than it sounds once you account for customs clearance on the last two shipments, both of which ran four days late through no fault of Belmoore's. There is no second qualified TiO2 supplier on the approved vendor list this year, the last candidate having failed a quality audit in March, and onboarding a new overseas supplier from scratch takes months the plant does not have. A single day of an idle coating line costs the plant a six figure sum in lost output, a number you have seen in a monthly report but never in a negotiation. That is not a fact you are meant to hand across the table.

What he does not know, or at least has not said, is that the plant's demand forecast for next year has actually dropped a little, since one of the export orders that used the most pigment intensive coating line got pushed to a competitor's facility over pricing you were not part of. Four hundred and eighty tons might be more volume than the plant will actually need next year. That gap, between what Belmoore assumes you need and what you actually need, is sitting in your notes. Alok has not asked about next year's production plan.

There is a second detail worth noticing before you decide anything. The formula he is proposing resets quarterly against an index that Belmoore itself contributes data to. Nobody in your file has flagged that. You are not sure yet whether that is a problem or just how these indices work.

---

**The Man Across the Table**

Alok is not a difficult man in the way training materials usually describe difficult men. He does not raise his voice. He smiles easily, remembers that you mentioned your hometown once at a plant tour last year, and asks after it before getting back to business. He clicks the cap of his pen on and off while he waits for you to respond to something, a small habit that seems to get faster the longer a silence runs. But he interrupts. He restates his own opening position as though it were already agreed, moving straight from "here's what we need" to "so once we've settled the formula, let's talk timeline," skipping the part where you were supposed to have a say in the first. When you hesitate, he fills the silence for you. He offers to "make it simple" in ways that somehow never move Belmoore's ask.

Twenty minutes in, he checks his watch again and says, not unkindly, "I know you're new to this. I'm not trying to rush you. But we do need to land somewhere today. My flight's at six." He says it like a fact about the weather. Whether it is also something else is a question you cannot check from where you are sitting.

The outgoing file has more on it than the headline price. Volume is written as four hundred and eighty tons. The term has always been twelve months. Credit has sat at forty five days for as long as Belmoore has been on the vendor list; Belmoore has never asked for faster payment, and the plant has never offered it. None of that appears in Alok's opening pitch. He led with price and term. That is what he came in wanting to talk about.

Ritu's sticky note has one more line at the bottom you almost missed, half covered by a coffee ring: *He did this same push with the Nashik plant in March. Ask Priya what happened.* You know Priya only as a name on an old email chain, procurement lead at the sister plant. You have not called her yet. You are not sure there is time before Alok wants an answer, and you are not entirely sure what you would even ask.

---

**Now**

An hour in, Alok glances at his phone again, and this time does not hide it. "Half day," he says, almost to himself, then catches your eye and softens it into a smile. "Long one, I mean. Let's keep moving." The laptop is still open on his side of the table, the chart still on screen, cursor blinking over a cell that reads PROPOSED TERM in a font just slightly larger than everything around it. He closes the lid halfway, not all the way, and looks at you.

"So," he says. "Where do you want to start?"
