# Past the Pilot

Vivek Nair's assistant confirms the meeting twice in one email, which either means nothing or means everything, and you don't yet know which. The lobby downstairs has a wall of framed product packaging going back decades, chip bags and juice cartons in fonts that have clearly been redesigned every few years, and you find yourself reading the wall a little too closely while you wait for your visitor badge, mostly to have something to do with your eyes. The sign above the reception desk says Amberleaf Foods.

You are a Solutions Consultant at an analytics and data consulting firm, and you have forty-five minutes this afternoon to pitch a demand-forecasting engagement to a mid-size FMCG company that makes packaged snacks and beverages across a distribution network of several thousand retail outlets. The brief from your account manager was short: strong fit, competitive evaluation, budget already approved, close it if you can. You built a deck around exactly that brief, forecasting accuracy improvements, a live model walkthrough, a slide on integration timelines. You have run some version of this pitch a dozen times this quarter alone, and it has, more often than not, gone well. You know which slide gets the nod. You know which question usually comes right after the accuracy numbers, and you have a good answer ready for it. You had, until about six hours ago, no reason to think today would be any different from the last dozen.

You spent the drive over rehearsing the opening the way you always do, the same three lines that usually earn you the first genuine nod of a meeting. You're aware, sitting in the elevator now, that you haven't actually decided whether to use them.

---

**What the brief missed**

What the brief didn't mention, and what you only found out this morning from a colleague who used to cover a neighboring account, is that Amberleaf ran something close to this exact engagement eighteen months ago. A different analytics vendor, a demand-forecasting dashboard, built over four months, presented at a leadership town hall with real enthusiasm, the kind of launch that gets a mention in the company's internal newsletter. As far as your colleague knows, nobody in field sales opened it more than a handful of times after the first month. It was never formally canceled. It simply stopped coming up in conversation, the way projects do when everyone has quietly agreed not to mention them anymore.

Your colleague mentioned one more thing, almost as an aside, that you haven't been able to stop turning over since: Vivek Nair, the VP you're meeting this afternoon, was the internal champion who got that pilot funded, which means he is, if your colleague's read is right, someone who has already lived through a version of exactly what you're about to walk in and offer him.

Your colleague also mentioned, almost fondly, that Vivek used to personally walk the sales floor once a quarter, before the pandemic made that harder to keep up, just to hear directly from the reps rather than through a dashboard. Whether that habit survived is not something your colleague knew, but it stuck with you as a detail, a person who at some point cared enough about the gap between data and the field to go stand in it himself.

---

**What you believe about the work**

Your firm's model is genuinely strong on a technical level, and you believe that, not just because it's your job to believe it. Forecasting accuracy improvements in the range of fifteen to twenty percent over a naive baseline are realistic for a company with Amberleaf's data maturity. Their point-of-sale data is clean, their SKU catalogue is well structured, and the integration work is straightforward against systems you've connected to before. None of that is the part you're suddenly uncertain about, walking in.

What you're uncertain about is whether accuracy was ever actually the problem last time. A dashboard that nobody opens isn't failing on the math. It's failing somewhere between the math and the person standing in a retail outlet trying to decide how much stock to order for next week, and that gap, wherever it actually lives, is not a gap your standard forty-five-minute deck was built to address. You've run the accuracy slide a hundred times. You have never once had to answer the question of why a good forecast didn't change anyone's actual behavior on the ground, because you've never had to sit across from someone who watched that happen to his own project, in front of his own boss, with his own name on the recommendation.

You called an analyst on your own team this morning, someone who's worked more field-sales rollouts than you have, and asked her, bluntly, what actually kills adoption on projects like this when the model itself is fine. Her answer stuck with you on the drive over: nobody in a field job changes behavior because a number in a dashboard told them to. They change behavior because someone they trust, doing their actual job, tells them the number is worth trusting. If nobody built that bridge last time, the dashboard was always going to die alone, no matter how good the forecast was.

She added something else, almost as an afterthought, that you've been chewing on since: most failed analytics rollouts she's seen weren't killed by a single bad decision. They died from a hundred small ones, nobody assigned to own adoption, no one senior enough checking usage numbers after month one, a training session run once and never repeated for new hires. You don't yet know if any of that is Amberleaf's story. You only know it is a pattern worth listening for.

Your deck still has the accuracy slide. You have not deleted it. Sitting in the Amberleaf lobby, you catch yourself wondering whether leaving it in the bag is honesty about what your firm is good at, or a habit that will get you the same polite meeting everyone else got last time.

---

**What you don't know yet**

You don't know exactly what happened in the room when the previous pilot quietly died. You don't know if Vivek blames the vendor, blames the rollout, blames his own sales leadership for not enforcing adoption, or blames some combination he's never said out loud to anyone outside his own team. What you do know is that whatever story he's carrying into this meeting, it is almost certainly the real reason behind whatever objection he raises first, and it is very unlikely to be the objection he actually states.

You also know, because your account manager mentioned it in passing, that Vivek's own boss, the Chief Commercial Officer, signed off on the budget for that earlier pilot on Vivek's specific recommendation. That is not a small detail. A failed pilot that someone else championed is an unfortunate line item, forgotten by most people within a quarter. A failed pilot that you personally vouched for to your own boss is something closer to a mark against your judgment, one that doesn't fully go away just because eighteen months have passed and a new evaluation is underway, and one that would presumably make anyone in Vivek's position more careful the second time, not less.

The field sales team itself, the people who would actually be asked to use any new forecasting tool you sell into this account, has its own memory of the last attempt, one you have no direct access to and Vivek almost certainly does. You imagine, without any real evidence, that there's a version of the story that circulates among the regional sales managers whenever a new system gets proposed, something close to "remember the dashboard nobody used," said half as a joke and half as a warning to anyone tempted to get excited about the next one. Whatever skepticism exists on that floor right now is a fact of this deal whether or not it ever gets said out loud in your forty-five minutes.

Forty-five minutes felt like plenty of time this morning. It feels different now, walking down the corridor toward Vivek's office, the number less like a container for your deck and more like a countdown on something you haven't fully worked out how to open.

There is one more detail your colleague mentioned, delivered almost as a punchline: the previous vendor's dashboard, as far as anyone at Amberleaf will admit out loud, is still technically live. Still technically paid for, running out the remainder of a multi-year contract nobody had the appetite to unwind early. Somewhere in that building right now, a dashboard nobody opens is quietly generating a forecast nobody reads, and you don't yet know whether that fact will ever come up in your forty-five minutes, or whether raising it yourself would land as honesty or as an unnecessary provocation.

---

**Your stake**

Your own manager checked in an hour ago, mostly to confirm you were still on for the meeting, and added, almost as an afterthought, that this account has been flagged internally as a strategic logo for the quarter, the kind of deal leadership would notice closing and would also notice falling through. You didn't ask what "falling through" would look like on your side of things. You suspect you already know, and you suspect it looks a great deal like today's meeting going the way you're now afraid it might.

There's a quieter version of that stake too. You made your number last quarter for the first time since joining, and you were, for about a week, the version of yourself who believed the momentum was real rather than lucky. A logo this size going the wrong way in front of leadership would not erase that quarter, but it would certainly complicate the story you'd been quietly telling yourself about how this year was going to go.

---

**Vivek's office**

Vivek's office looks out over a car park rather than anything more impressive, which you decide not to read too much into. A framed photo on the side table shows what looks like a company town hall from a couple of years back, a packed room, someone at a podium mid-sentence, and you find yourself wondering, uselessly, whether that's the exact town hall where the last dashboard got its big launch. He shakes your hand, offers water you decline, and sits down across a small round table rather than behind his desk, a choice that could mean he wants this to feel informal or could mean nothing at all. He glances once at the laptop bag on your shoulder before you've even set it down.

The conversation is about to start. Your next line will decide whether this is another accuracy deck he has already survived, or the first honest forty-five minutes he has had about why the last one died.
