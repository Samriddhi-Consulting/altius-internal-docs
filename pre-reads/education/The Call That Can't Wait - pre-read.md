# The Call That Can't Wait

The call is in ten minutes and you still have not decided how to start it. Your headset is lying on the desk where you dropped it an hour ago. The meeting invite on your screen still says "Weekly Sync," which was an honest name for this call right up until about forty minutes ago.

You are a project coordinator at a mid-size services firm, six months into your first job. For the last two weeks you have been the point of contact on a delivery that was supposed to go out to a client this Thursday. It is not going out this Thursday. You found out for certain forty minutes ago, when the vendor your team depends on for one final piece of the work confirmed, apologetically, that they need another week.

You believed them the last time they said that. You believed them the time before that as well, which is part of why this call sits heavier than it should for something that, on paper, is not your fault.

Forty minutes is long enough to do several small useful things and nowhere near long enough to feel ready for the large one. You confirmed the new timeline with the vendor twice, because the first confirmation came back too fast to trust. You asked your delivery lead whether anything at all could go out partially this week to take the edge off the miss. He looked at it properly, which you appreciated, and said no, and then said it again more gently when you asked a second time. You drafted three emails and deleted all three. You were most of the way into a fourth when your manager stopped at your desk on her way past.

"That one needs a call," she said. "Not an email."

You already knew. You had been hoping somebody else would say it out loud so that it would not have to be your decision.

---

**What Your Manager Said On Her Way Back To Her Desk**

"You're not going to feel ready. Nobody feels ready for this the first few times." She was already half turned toward her own desk, mug in one hand. "Say the actual thing early, then listen more than you talk. That's really it."

It was a short conversation. It was meant to be reassuring and it mostly was, although the reassurance appears to have a shelf life, and the shelf life appears to be roughly half an hour.

She told you something else earlier in the week, more casually than the information deserved. This account is flagged internally as a relationship worth protecting. Not because of what it bills today. Because of what it could turn into if this project lands the way everybody expects it to. Then she said "you've got this" with the specific brightness of a person who trusts you and has no intention of joining the call.

There is a smaller version of that stake and you have not said it out loud to anyone. This is the first account she handed you completely. Nobody shadowing the calls. Nobody reading your emails before they go out. You know that was a bet. You also know that bets get quietly reconsidered in conversations you are not in the room for. Six months is not long enough to have banked much benefit of the doubt on something this visible, and however the next fifteen minutes go, they will say more about you inside your own firm than the vendor's slipped timeline ever will.

---

**Two Weeks Of Being The One Who Answers**

The delivery has been running longer than you have been on it. You picked the account up two weeks ago, when the coordinator before you moved onto something else, and the handover was a forty-minute call and access to a shared folder. What you inherited was a project most of the way through, one outstanding dependency, and a client who had already been given a date twice.

Since then you have been the person who answers. You send Priyanka a short status note on Tuesday mornings, four or five lines, no attachment, because she said early on that she reads short things properly and files long ones for later. You have sent six of those notes. Every one of them has said the work is on track, and every one of them was written from what the vendor had told your delivery lead that week.

You have picked up things about the vendor in two weeks that nobody put in the handover. They reply quickly and they estimate warmly. When they say a piece of work is nearly done, "nearly" has stretched to cover anything from two days to a fortnight. Nobody at your firm treats this as a scandal. It is simply the shape of working with them, and that shape is now sitting inside a commitment somebody made to a client who built a launch behind it.

More than once in the last forty minutes you have thought that you should have seen this coming. You have also thought that there was no realistic way you could have, and that both of those can be true at once without either one letting you off. Neither thought has been much use.

Nine minutes.

---

**The Third Date**

This is the third date for the same delivery.

The first slip was three days. Small enough that you folded it into a routine check-in, and she barely paused before moving on to the next item on her list. It cost nothing. It is probably the reason the second one felt survivable.

The second slip was a week and a half. Priyanka Oswal took that one well enough on the surface. She asked two or three practical questions, wrote something down, said "fine, that's workable," and then, near the end of the call, when you had already relaxed, asked whether the new date was "actually real this time."

There was no heat in it. She asked the way you would ask a mechanic whether the car was actually fixed. She asked the way somebody asks a question they have decided to ask only once.

You said yes.

You believed it when you said it, and that is the part you keep circling back to this afternoon. You were not managing her. You were repeating what you had been told by people who also believed it, and the whole chain of belief has now failed twice in the same direction.

You are calling to tell her it has failed a third time.

Somewhere between that call and this one you stopped thinking about this as a scheduling problem. You could not name the moment it changed.

---

**Priyanka**

Priyanka Oswal is Head of Operations at Halcyon Retail Brands, and you have worked with her for about two months. That is long enough to know how she runs a call and nowhere near long enough to know what she does when she has had enough of something.

She has a marketing launch built around this delivery. Her team has been sequencing work behind it for weeks, and her dates went upward on her side of the wall the same way your dates went upward on yours. She mentioned once, in passing, that her own boss has started asking about this project by name in their weekly one-on-one. People do not usually say that as a complaint. They say it the way you mention weather that has started to look like it might do something.

She has not escalated. Two slips, and there has been no complaint email to your manager, no demand for a credit or a discount, no call to somebody senior at your firm who could have made your fortnight considerably worse. Nothing about this account would show up on any dashboard at your end as a risk. From the outside, this relationship still looks completely fine.

You are aware, without being able to prove it from anything she has actually said, that the outside of a relationship and the inside of one can be quite different, and that restraint is not the same thing as acceptance.

There is a small detail from your first call with her that you keep thinking about today. She mentioned, almost as an aside, that she blocks her Friday afternoons to properly read status reports instead of skimming them in a meeting, because she would rather catch a problem in week two than be handed it in week six. At the time it struck you as slightly old fashioned and slightly admirable. It reads differently now, knowing that whatever she takes from this call, she will have given it her actual attention rather than half of it.

And there was the end of that same first call, when she stayed on the line four minutes past the point where the agenda had run out, explaining what the launch actually was and why her team cared about it, because, she said, it helps to know what you are building toward. She was walking somewhere while she talked. You could hear a door, and then a lift, and then her saying she had to go.

---

**What You Are Carrying Into The Call**

The problem itself is not complicated, which is either a comfort or the opposite.

The vendor dependency is a known, bounded thing. Roughly a week to resolve. Not an open-ended mystery, not a rewrite, not a discovery that something fundamental was wrong. Your delivery lead is confident enough in that estimate that he offered, unprompted, to put the revised timeline in writing, and from him that counts as a strong statement.

You know all of this. Priyanka knows none of it yet.

You have rehearsed one opening line four or five times now. Some version of "I wanted to give you an update on where things stand." Every time you hear it back it sounds like the start of a sentence built to soften a blow rather than deliver one, and you are still tempted by it, because the alternative feels like walking into the call already on the back foot. Your mouth seems to produce it without being asked.

There is a second thing you have not worked out. Priyanka does not work with your vendor. She has never spoken to anybody there, does not know their name, and could not hold them to anything even if she wanted to. Whatever is true about who caused this delay, the version she has lived through is simpler than the version you have: a date you gave her, twice, that did not hold either time.

Eight minutes. Somebody two desks over laughs at something on another call, entirely unaware that the next quarter of an hour matters more to you than anything else that has happened this week.

---

**Six Minutes**

You notice you have stopped rehearsing sentences and started simply breathing, which might be progress or might be your nerve running out of material to work with.

You reread the vendor's email one last time and then close it, because you do not want to be looking at it while she is talking. You check that the new date is written down somewhere you can see without scrolling. You move your water glass, for no reason you could defend.

The office carries on exactly as it did five minutes ago. Somebody is arguing pleasantly with a printer. Your manager is at her own desk with her back to you, which is either tact or a coincidence.

Your phone buzzes. The call window is open. You have a few seconds to decide whether to dial in with a first line already chosen, or to let the first thing you say be whatever comes out once she picks up, and you are aware, with slightly more clarity than is comfortable, that you are deciding which kind of person you are going to be on this call.

She picks up on the second ring. Brisk, already mid-thought, somewhere in the middle of whatever she was doing thirty seconds ago.
