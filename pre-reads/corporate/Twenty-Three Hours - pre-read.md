# Twenty-Three Hours

The call was meant to run twenty minutes. It was a standing quarterly review with Pashan Financial, the kind you take with one eye on your inbox, and for the first eleven minutes it went exactly the way those calls go. Then Sridevi Menon, who runs pricing at Pashan and who has said kind things about your firm from a stage twice in the last two years, stopped talking about the current quarter and said she wanted to go back to something.

She shared her screen. On it was a slide from her own board pack, dated March, showing Pashan's fee position against its peer set. Then she pulled up the same view from the product as it stands today. The two numbers were not close. Not a rounding difference, not a definitional wrinkle you could talk through. A gap wide enough that the strategic conclusion her board had drawn in March pointed the other way now.

"I have been reporting this internally for two quarters," she said. Her voice was level, which was worse than if it had not been. "I need to understand what happened, and I need to know what you are going to do about it. I have a board sub-committee on Thursday morning. So end of day tomorrow, please."

Then she thanked everyone for their time and left the call, and the six of you sat in the silence that follows that kind of exit.

You lead a team of six inside Benchmarks. Your product is the comparative benchmarking suite, the thing banks and large merchants subscribe to so they can see where they sit against everyone else in their category. Pashan is one of your three largest revenue lines. The suite renews in January. Nobody has said the word renewal out loud yet, and the fact that nobody has said it is doing something to the air in the room.

By four in the afternoon you knew roughly what had happened, which is not the same as knowing what to do. A data source changed upstream at the start of the year. The change was documented, approved, and unremarkable on its own terms. What nobody traced through was that it shifted the composition of the peer groups underneath the product. The bank Pashan was being compared against in March is not quite the bank it is being compared against now. Every number the product has produced since is internally consistent and externally irreconcilable with everything it produced before.

Two of your analysts have been on the reconciliation since last evening. They have got nowhere useful. Your own vertical head is somewhere over the Arabian Sea with her phone off and will not land for another six hours.

Which is how the escalation went around her and landed on Anand Rajagopal's desk instead.

Anand runs the whole intelligence function, all four verticals, the thing that was assembled fourteen months ago out of teams that until then had barely shared a floor. Benchmarks, where you sit. Flows, who do consumer and category spend analytics. Strategy, who package all of it into advisory work built for one client at a time. And Signals, who build the proprietary models and scores that get sold into bank risk teams.

You have been at the firm four years. You could not, if pressed, name three people in Signals.

There is a reason for that and it is not unfriendliness. Signals sells into bank risk officers. You sell into strategy and pricing teams. In four years the two of you have never once been in the same client meeting, and the merger did not change that, because the merger changed the reporting lines and the seating chart and left everything else exactly where it was. You share a floor, a coffee machine and a town hall. That is the whole relationship.

Anand called you at half past five. He did not ask what went wrong, which you noticed and were grateful for. He asked what you needed. Then he said something you were not expecting.

"Signals had this. Not this exactly. Something in the same family, about a year ago, on one of the risk scores. A model refresh moved the reference population underneath live client scores and their clients had already acted on the old ones." A pause. "They got out of it in about a week and a half. Cleanly, from what I remember. Talk to Meher Vashisht."

You wrote the name down.

"I have messaged her," Anand said. "She will make time tonight. Look, I am not worried about this. You have twenty-four hours and someone in this building has already solved it. Just go and ask her."

Somewhere in there is the thing that makes this harder than he thinks it is.

You have never met Meher Vashisht. You have never met anyone on her team. When you looked her up on the internal directory you found a photograph, a title that told you almost nothing, and a reporting line that goes up through Signals and across to Anand the same way yours does. Eleven people in her pod. She has led it for about eighteen months, since the reorg dropped her into the role. Before that she was one of the engineers.

You also found, on an old internal blog post from before the merger, a photograph of her team standing awkwardly around a cake. The caption said something about a release. She is at the edge of the frame, not quite in it, holding a plate.

What you do not have is any idea what she has been told, beyond a two-line message from the head of the function on a Tuesday morning telling her to help someone she has never heard of with something that has not been explained to her.

Think about how that message reads from her side. Not as help. As exposure.

She does not know what you want. She does not know whether the thing you are about to ask her for is something she is allowed to give you, or something that was fine inside her own team a year ago and becomes a different object entirely the moment it leaves the room. She does not know whether saying yes to you means saying yes to every other vertical that has a data problem from here on. She has eleven people and no headcount for that.

And she knows, because everyone knows, that when something goes wrong at this firm the question that gets asked is not what did we learn. It is who owned it.

The clock does not care about any of this. It is twenty past nine in the evening. Sridevi's deadline is end of day tomorrow, which you are choosing to read as six in the evening rather than midnight, because you will need whatever you find to survive a review by your own vertical head before it goes anywhere near Pashan. Twenty-three hours, give or take, and most of them are hours in which you will be asleep or in other meetings.

Here is what you actually have.

The failure mechanism you understand. The peer-group composition shifted between the December and March production runs. You can demonstrate it, you can date it, and you can show that no single person did anything wrong. That last part is true and it will be worth exactly nothing to Sridevi if it arrives without a remediation path attached.

What you do not have is any workable answer to the question underneath hers, which is what happens to two quarters of numbers her board has already acted on. Your options as they currently stand are all bad. You can issue a formal correction, which is clean, defensible, slow, and would require the firm to publicly restate a product output for the first time anyone can remember. You can rebuild the historical peer groups to the old definition and run everything again, which your analysts estimate at three to four weeks and which does not exist by tomorrow evening. Or you can go to Sridevi with an explanation and no path, which is the option where you find out how much goodwill two conference-stage endorsements actually buy.

Signals faced the third version of this and came out in nine or ten days without any of those three things happening. You do not know how. You do not know what they built, or whether it was a technical fix or a commercial one or something in between. Anand called it clean. Anand was also not in the room.

You have a video call booked with Meher at ten. She has already accepted it, which tells you something, though not much. She is taking it from home.

You have spent the last hour building a list of questions. Reading it back now it looks like an incident review. What was the root cause. How did you identify the affected clients. What was the remediation. Who signed off. It is a good list for a post-mortem and you have a growing feeling that it is the wrong list for tonight, because every question on it invites her to defend her team, and a person defending her team gives you process. You do not need process. You need the thing they actually did.

There is a version of this call where you open by saying Anand asked you to speak with her. It would be true. It would also be the single most efficient way to turn her into someone who answers in complete, careful, useless sentences.

And there is a version where you get everything you need out of her in forty minutes, hang up, fix your product, and never speak to her again. Your product would be fixed. You would also have spent a piece of something the function does not have much of, fourteen months after somebody decided these four teams should be one thing.

There is one more thing, and you have been trying not to look at it directly all evening. Whatever comes out of tomorrow, someone is going to write it up. Your vertical head will land, read the reconciliation, and want to know why the escalation reached Anand before it reached her. Your name is on the client relationship. Your name is on the March production run sign-off, halfway down a list of four, in a system nobody has opened since. None of that is unfair. It is just true, and it will still be true in a week regardless of how the next hour goes.

At two minutes to ten you close the reconciliation file. The office is nearly empty. One of your analysts is still at her desk with headphones on, doing something to a spreadsheet that you suspect is no longer productive, and you tell her to go home, and she says she will, and you both know she will not.

You open the call and sit with your own face in the corner of the screen for a moment, and you do the thing you always do, which is look at yourself and immediately look away.

At ten exactly the tile flips over and there she is. A kitchen behind her, a light on over a counter, something on the hob. Her hair is tied back. She is holding a mug in both hands and she does not put it down.
